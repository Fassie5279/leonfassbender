Why I Love Imperfect Photos

Leon Fassbender
Take a look at the three unedited photos below.
 
 
 
 
Technically speaking, none of them are "perfect." They aren't sharp—at least not in that hyper-crisp, digital way we’re used to seeing all over social media and in magazines these days. There's a reason for that. I shot these photos on old film cameras using ISO 100 film. This forced me to use slower shutter speeds, introducing less depth of field and a bit of micro camera shake. On top of that, vintage lenses simply don't have that clinical, razor-sharp edge that modern lenses do.
 
But you know what? I absolutely love these little imperfections.
To me, the lack of clinical sharpness is exactly what gives these images their soul, energy, and character. There is an organic warmth to them, and I just know they would look incredible printed the traditional way in a darkroom.
 
The great Henri Cartier-Bresson once called sharpness a "bourgeois concept," and I couldn't agree more. I'm not just echoing his words, either—I’ve lived by this exact same philosophy for over twenty years, long before I ever came across his famous quote.
Don't get me wrong, there is a time and a place for technical perfection. When doing a commercial commission, shooting products, or capturing interiors, the images need to be pin-sharp. Period. I completely get that. But at its heart, photography isn't about chasing sharpness, tweaking color profiles, or slapping on digital filters to manufacture a certain "look." It’s not about the gear, and it's definitely not about the technical specs of the camera. To me, photography is a language to share an emotion, a mood, and above all, a feeling. And honestly, I usually find a lot more feeling in imperfection. After all, what’s the point of a technically flawless photo if it doesn't make you feel anything?
As Daido Moriyama once beautifully pointed out, you can write the most breathtaking poem with the cheapest ballpoint pen. His argument: It’s never about the tools you hold - it’s about the story you want to tell.
 
I believe there's beauty in simplicity, especially when it comes to photography - and I'll go a step further and say it applies to all art forms. There's a profound power in understatement; in leaving something unsaid so that the message resonates louder and clearer. That is exactly what these technically unsharp photos do for me, and it's why they possess such incredible staying power. And I'm not alone in this. It is the exact reason why we still stare in awe at a man leaping over a puddle in Paris, or American soldiers struggling through the crashing waves of Omaha Beach. Those iconic pictures aren't sharp, and because they aren't, they force the mind and the heart to sharpen instead - compelling us to truly feel the story.
 
That is why a raw, imperfect photograph will always triumph over the clinical world of over-edited, hyper-focused, digital-color-profile-infested camera recipes. You can engineer perfection, but you cannot manufacture feeling.

Long live imperfection.
